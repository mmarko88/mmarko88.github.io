Agentic Development Experts and Resources: Who to Follow and What to Study

Oct 22, 2026 · 7 min read · agents ai resources community  ·

When I started building agent skills, I made every mistake from scratch. Months later I found that most of them were documented — by researchers, by tool builders, by practitioners who'd already burned the same hours. The problem wasn't lack of information; it was not knowing where to look.

This post is the reading list I wish I'd had on day one.

The one thing to take from this post: the agentic development field moves fast, but the people doing the most useful work are surprisingly consistent. Follow the practitioners who publish their failures, not just their wins.

The researchers

These are the people producing the foundational work on why agents behave the way they do — the papers that explain what you're seeing in practice.

Shunyu Yao

Where: Twitter/X (@ShunyuYao12), Papers on arXiv

Author of ReAct (Reasoning + Acting) — the paper that formalized the loop most coding agents use today: think → act → observe → think. If your agent alternates between reasoning and tool calls, it's doing ReAct whether it knows it or not.

Also co-authored Tree of Thoughts and contributed to agent evaluation frameworks. His work is dense but foundational — worth reading at least the ReAct abstract to understand why agents reason between tool calls instead of planning everything upfront.

Key paper: ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models (ICLR 2023)

Andrew Ng

Where: deeplearning.ai, Twitter/X (@AndrewYNg), The Batch newsletter

Popularized the term agentic workflows and published a clear taxonomy of agent design patterns: reflection, tool use, planning, and multi-agent collaboration. His short courses on DeepLearning.AI are the fastest path from "I've heard of agents" to "I understand the architecture."

His framework for thinking about agentic patterns (reflection, tool use, planning, multi-agent) is the mental model most practitioners use, even if they don't cite it explicitly.

Key resource: Agentic Design Patterns talk series (free on DeepLearning.AI)

Huang et al. (self-correction research)

Where: arXiv, ICLR proceedings

The team behind the "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet" paper. This is the research that explains why having an agent review its own output doesn't work — the finding I wrote about in Why Your Coding Agent Needs a Different Critic.

If you only read one paper on agent limitations, make it this one. It saves you from building a self-review system that feels like it should work but doesn't.

Key paper: Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet (ICLR 2024)

The practitioners

These are people building and shipping with coding agents, publishing what actually works (and what doesn't).

Simon Willison

Where: simonwillison.net, Twitter/X (@simonw), Mastodon

The most prolific documentor of LLM tool usage in practice. Creator of llm (CLI tool for interacting with language models) and datasette. His blog posts are meticulous experiments: he tries something, measures it, reports the result including failures.

What makes his work valuable for agent skill building: he treats prompts as engineering artifacts. His posts on system prompts, tool definitions, and structured output are directly applicable to skill writing.

Start here: His posts tagged "prompt engineering" and "Claude" — they're practical experiments, not theory.

Swyx (Shawn Wang)

Where: latent.space (podcast + newsletter), Twitter/X (@swyx)

Runs the Latent Space podcast — the best source for long-form interviews with people building AI tools. His AI engineer framework categorizes the landscape: infrastructure, models, and applications. For agent skills specifically, his episodes with tool builders (Cursor, Devin, Cline, Windsurf teams) give insider perspective on how these tools expect skills/rules to be written.

Also coined the concept of "AI Engineer" as a distinct role — useful framing for understanding where agent skill building fits in the development workflow.

Start here: Latent Space podcast episodes with coding agent builders.

Thorsten Ball

Where: registerspill.thorstenball.com, Twitter/X (@thorstenball)

Thorsten works at Zed (the editor) on their AI integration and writes candidly about what works and what doesn't when embedding AI into developer tools. His posts on context window management and prompt design are grounded in real product experience.

Start here: His blog posts on building AI features in Zed.

Ethan Mollick

Where: oneusefulthing.org, Twitter/X (@emollick)

Wharton professor studying how AI changes work. Not a coding-agent specialist, but his research on how humans and AI collaborate effectively applies directly. His work on delegation patterns, verification habits, and the "jagged frontier" of AI capability helps you understand when to use an agent and when to code it yourself.

Start here: Co-Intelligence (book) and his Substack posts on AI at work.

Harper Reed

Where: harper.blog, Twitter/X (@harper)

Former CTO of Obama's 2012 campaign and Threadless. Published practical guides on using AI agents for software development. His approach to prompt crafting and project planning with agents is pragmatic — focused on what ships rather than what's theoretically possible.

Start here: His posts on AI-assisted development workflows.

Open-source skill repositories and references

Cursor Directory

URL: cursor.directory

Community-curated collection of Cursor rules for different frameworks and languages. This is the closest thing to a "skill marketplace" that exists today. Browse it for:

  • Rules for your specific framework (Spring Boot, React, Django, etc.)
  • Patterns for how other teams structure their rules
  • Ideas for rules you haven't thought of

Awesome CursorRules

URL: github.com/PatrickJS/awesome-cursorrules

Curated GitHub repo of Cursor rule files. More opinionated than cursor.directory — the maintainers filter for quality. Good for studying how experienced users structure their rules.

Anthropic's prompt engineering guide

URL: docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering

Anthropic's official guide on how to write effective prompts for Claude. Since many coding agents run on Claude, this is effectively the "how to write skills" manual from the model provider. Pay special attention to the sections on:

  • System prompts (directly applicable to rules/skills)
  • Tool use (how the model decides which tool to call)
  • Chain of thought (when to ask for reasoning vs direct output)

OpenAI's prompt engineering guide

URL: platform.openai.com/docs/guides/prompt-engineering

Similar to Anthropic's guide but for GPT models. Useful if your agent runs on OpenAI. The tactics on structured output and few-shot examples translate directly to skill writing.

ExpeL and Agent Workflow Memory (academic)

Two papers worth reading if you want to understand how agent skills could eventually self-improve:

  • ExpeL (arXiv:2308.10144) — Extract reusable insights from agent trajectories. The idea: after each session, automatically identify patterns that worked and codify them as rules.
  • Agent Workflow Memory (arXiv:2409.07429) — Induce commonly-reused routines from past action sequences. Similar concept: mine the agent's own history for skill candidates.

These are research directions, not production tools yet. But they describe where the field is heading — agents that build their own skills from experience.

A learning path

If you're starting from zero and want to be productive with agent skills in two weeks:

Week 1: Foundations

  1. Read Andrew Ng's Agentic Design Patterns overview (1 hour)
  2. Read Anthropic's prompt engineering guide, system prompts section (30 min)
  3. Set up 5-10 project rules in your tool of choice (30 min)
  4. Run 3-5 sessions with the rules active, noting where the agent still fails

Week 2: Skills 5. Read the previous post in this series for skill templates 6. Write your first investigate skill based on a real failure from Week 1 7. Write a review/critic skill 8. Browse cursor.directory for rules specific to your stack 9. Read 2-3 of Simon Willison's experiment posts for calibration

After two weeks you'll have working rules and skills, a reading pipeline for staying current, and — most importantly — your own measured experience of what works for your specific codebase.

That's worth more than any expert's recommendation, including this one.
