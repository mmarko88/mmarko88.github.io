# What are the current best practices for posting on LinkedIn (to promote content and grow reach)?

**Answer:** Post niche, expert-level content 2–4 times per week in a consistent voice; make every post self-sufficient (a conclusion, number, or lesson in the text itself) with a strong first line, because dwell time and "see more" clicks are core ranking signals. Put substance before format — images and well-formatted text both work; comments, saves, and shares outweigh likes by a wide margin. External links do cost reach (estimates range ~19–60%, unverifiable because LinkedIn documents no penalty), and the "link in first comment" workaround is now penalized too — so either accept the trade for clicks or keep links rare and contextual.

**Confidence:** Medium-high. The strategic direction (expertise, relevance, dwell time, meaningful comments) comes from LinkedIn's own team and is consistent across sources. Specific percentages come almost entirely from third-party observational studies — especially one vendor, Richard van der Blom — and are unreproducible.

## Why

**The algorithm optimizes for relevance and expertise, not virality.** LinkedIn's product team states the feed ranks on three signals — relevance to a defined audience, demonstrated expertise, and meaningful engagement — and explicitly deprioritized viral content after 2023. Posts are shown to first-degree connections first; distribution expands when "knowledge-rich" content earns engagement [1][2]. The March 2026 feed update targets repetitive low-substance posts, engagement bait, and comment automation [3]. The engineering blog confirms time spent on a post — including whether people scroll past without stopping — feeds the ranking system [4].

**Dwell time and substance are the mechanical levers.** "See more" expansion clicks, saves, substantive comments, and private/cross-platform shares carry several times the weight of likes, per van der Blom's 1.8M-post analysis; formatted posts performed ~2x better in his data, attributed to dwell time [5][6]. Independent practitioner data agrees on direction: posts of 1,500–3,000 characters earned the highest engagement rates (2.10%) in one dataset, and candid, personal, journey-style posts outperformed polished how-tos in Buffer's controlled 30-day experiment [7][8]. The mechanism matters more than the number: a reader who stops, expands, and replies is the signal — so the first line (the hook before the fold) does disproportionate work [2][8].

**Comments are the highest-leverage activity — yours and others'.** Comments spawn threads that extend dwell time and train the interest graph; LinkedIn's own guidance is to write posts that invite genuine replies and to tag people only when relevant [2]. Van der Blom's data adds that comments from 2nd-degree connections carry ~2.6x the distribution impact of 1st-degree, and that AI-generated and pod-style comments can suppress a post's growth — consistent with LinkedIn's stated crackdown on comment automation [3][6].

**External links: real cost, unknown size, dead workaround.** LinkedIn has never documented a link penalty; every percentage is third-party observation of an opaque system [3][9]. The direction is nonetheless consistent across independent observers: van der Blom measured ~40% lower reach for a single outbound link (Oct 2024), and the skeptical audits agree link posts underperform while disputing that the *link itself* is the cause — thin "title + URL" posts give readers nothing to engage with [6][9][10]. The resolution: both are true at once. The link correlates with reach loss partly because link posts are usually promotional and thin; a substantive post with a link loses less. Meanwhile the classic workaround is stale advice — as of mid-2026, van der Blom's data shows links in comments penalized up to ~80% and converting 3.4x worse than the same link in the body, and LinkedIn actively deprioritizes link-bearing comments [9]. Multiple contextual links may actually cost less (~9%) than a single focal link (~18.8%), suggesting the penalty targets link-as-destination, not link-as-citation [9].

**Cadence and timing are second-order but real.** Posts earn most of their views within ~24 hours, so 2–3 posts/week beats 1/week for sustained visibility (+57% engagement per post in one dataset) [7]; Buffer's experiment shows consistency itself compounds — average impressions rose 46% by week four of steady posting [8]. Broad timing benchmarks: weekday mornings, especially Tuesday–Wednesday 8–9 AM, perform best; weekends are weakest; technology audiences peak around Monday 11 AM [11]. These are starting points, not laws — audience timezone and habits dominate.

**Format advantages rotate; don't chase them.** Image posts led impressions and text led comments in Buffer's n=25 experiment; carousels had the lowest engagement rate there despite industry hype; video reach reportedly dropped sharply in early 2025 after a boost period [6][8]. LinkedIn's team recommends ≤3 relevant hashtags, relevant keywords, and no engagement bait [2].

## What would change this

- LinkedIn publishing an explicit statement that outbound links carry no ranking penalty — currently every penalty figure is external inference [3][10].
- A paired, within-author controlled test showing link posts underperform *after* holding post quality constant; if the gap vanished, the "penalty" is a content-quality confound, and the advice reduces to "write substantive posts" [10].
- Evidence the comment-link penalty was reversed or is not author-aware — it rests on a single vendor's unreleased methodology [9].

## Considered and rejected

- **Link in first comment.** Formerly standard advice; now penalized (~80% reach loss reported) and converts 3.4x worse than an in-post link. Stale, not wrong at the time [9].
- **Daily posting / chasing virality.** Viral reach was explicitly deprioritized by LinkedIn; frequency helps only up to sustained quality, and thin daily posts are exactly what the 2026 update targets [2][3].
- **Engagement bait, pods, mass tagging, AI comments.** All measured or stated to suppress reach; the algorithm now detects coordinated and automated engagement [2][3][6].
- **Optimizing for likes.** Likes are among the weakest signals; saves, comments, and shares dominate [2][5].
- **Hashtag stacking.** More than ~3 relevant hashtags adds nothing; hashtags are downgraded as a discovery signal [2][6].

## Open

- The true size of the link penalty after controlling for post quality — no published study holds format and substance constant. Resolvable only by a paired test on your own account (4–6 weeks, alternating link placement, tracking clicks not just impressions) [9][10].
- Whether multi-link posts genuinely cost less reach than single-link posts — single-source finding [9].
- Whether video's reach decline is durable or a temporary rebalance — single source, 3-month window [6].
